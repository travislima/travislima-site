When I joined WooCommerce, the sales and customer success function I'm part of barely existed. We built it from zero — and it now drives a meaningful part of Woo's new and recurring revenue.

That experience settled a debate for me that a lot of companies are still having: **customer success is not a cost center you tolerate. It's a growth engine you underfund at your own risk.**

## The math nobody argues with (but everybody ignores)

Everyone can recite the statistic that retaining a customer is cheaper than acquiring one. Almost nobody staffs like they believe it. Acquisition gets the budget, the dashboards, and the headcount; retention gets a shared inbox.

But look at where growth actually comes from in any subscription or platform business: expansion, renewal, and referral — all downstream of the customer succeeding. A merchant who scales from $1M to $5M on your platform is worth more than almost any new logo you'll close this year. And they only do that if someone is paying attention to whether they're succeeding.

## What customer success actually is

The name undersells it. The job isn't answering tickets — it's building a bridge between what the customer is trying to do and what the platform can do. In practice that means:

- **Hearing the problem behind the question.** "How do I export orders?" is rarely about exports. It's about a reporting gap, a fulfillment mess, or a migration someone is quietly considering.
- **Carrying the customer's voice inward.** The roadmap conversations that matter most start with "three of my merchants hit this wall this month."
- **Solving before escalation.** By the time something becomes a churn conversation, the window was months ago. Success work is early work.

## For merchants: this applies to your store too

The same logic runs inside an ecommerce business. Your repeat customers are your growth engine — and most stores treat post-purchase the way companies treat support: as an afterthought. The stores I see scale flip that. They invest in the second order like most stores invest in the first.

If you're deciding where your next hire or your next hour goes: acquisition is rented growth. Retention is owned growth. Build the engine you own.
