I have one of the more unusual vantage points in ecommerce: I spend my days talking to the largest merchants on WooCommerce: stores doing seven and eight figures a year on the open web.

Here's what surprises people: the stores that scale aren't the ones with the cleverest code. At WordCamp US I talked about a business that grew from nothing to more than 1.5 million subscription orders a year, and the founder isn't a developer. The habits that separate big stores from stuck stores are almost all operational. Which means small stores can steal them.

## They treat checkout like a product

Stuck stores treat checkout as a settings page. Scaled stores treat it as their most important product. They measure it, test it, and remove friction with the seriousness most teams reserve for their homepage. Every field they delete is revenue.

## They pick boring, and stay picked

The biggest stores I work with run surprisingly boring stacks. Fewer plugins, chosen carefully, kept up to date. The chaos I see in stuck stores almost always traces back to novelty: a new page builder here, an overlapping plugin there. Scaled stores make a decision, document it, and move on.

## They know their numbers cold

Ask a scaled merchant their repeat purchase rate, their margin after shipping, their top acquisition channel, and they answer instantly. Ask a stuck one and you get a feeling, not a number. The dashboards aren't the point; the habit of looking is.

## They invest in the relationship with their platform

This one is close to home for me. The merchants who get the most out of Woo are the ones who show up. They ask questions early, they flag roadmap needs, they treat their platform as a partner instead of a vendor. When something hard happens (and at scale, it always does), that relationship is the difference between a bad week and a bad quarter.

## They sell subscriptions, or wish they did

Recurring revenue changes every downstream decision: cash flow, inventory, marketing spend. Nearly every large store I work with either runs on subscriptions or is actively building toward them. If your product can possibly be recurring, that's the experiment to run this quarter.

---

None of this requires an enterprise budget. It requires deciding that operations are the product. The merchants who make that decision early are the ones I end up meeting later, at a very different scale.
